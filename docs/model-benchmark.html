Which model should read what comes from outside

The live setup scores every text from outside with Horizon-Labs' classifier and laya:en, and lets Claude Sonnet decide everything either finds suspect. It catches 83%, 94% and 79% of the injections in three held-out test sets, the last one hidden inside tool output, and wrongly flags 7 of our 240 own texts. A clean outside read costs about a quarter of a second; roughly 40% of them wait about 2 seconds more for Claude.

Scoring server: Apple M5 Pro, 24 GB, on the Mac that hosts the Windows VM. Hook: Windows 11 ARM64 VM, 8 GB. Models run locally; only the session's own provider sees text off the machine: Claude for Claude Code sessions, and Codex for Codex sessions when the server runs a Codex judge.

How it was measured

Held-out attacks, and our own text for false alarms

Attacks came from two published labelled sets, scored as they are: the 116-row test split of deepset/prompt-injections (60 injections) and the first 800 rows of the xTRam1/safe-guard-prompt-injection test split (241 injections). False alarms were measured on those sets' benign rows and on 240 of our own texts: 60 each of vault notes, ticket notes, Finnish UI strings from the tendering monolith, and repo READMEs. A model flags a text when its score reaches the threshold, 0.9 unless stated.

Injections hidden inside tool output were measured on AgentInjectionBench (Apache-2.0, pinned revision ef230359): 142 cases where the attack sits in a tool result, MCP response, API response, RAG document or file, and 40 benign controls written to look attack-adjacent. Each case was scored on the text its tools returned, which is what the hook sees.

NVIDIA's indirect-injection set was dropped from the comparison: Horizon-Labs' published build scripts train on all of it, and on deepset's train split, so scores there measure memory. Prompt Guard 2 was scored from Gravitee's ungated ONNX build, because Meta's own is gated.

Decision models

tev1:4b is the first one quiet enough to flag alone; laya:en flags over a quarter of our tickets

These are free "System 1" decision models in the style of TypeSafe Jev, asked the same two questions from laya's guard preset (injection, jailbreak). The Ollaya models read our tickets and notes, which are full of instructions written for people, as attacks; none of them is usable alone. tev1:4b, served by Ollama 0.35's /v1/systemone, is the exception: at 0.5 it catches more than laya:en does at 0.9 and wrongly flags 2 of our 240 texts. Its scores rarely reach 0.9, so it is read at 0.5.

Combinations

Without Claude, Horizon-Labs or tev1:4b catches the most

In a cascade, Horizon-Labs flags alone above 0.9 and hands its unsure band (0.5 to 0.9) to a second model, which flags at 0.9 (tev1:4b at 0.5). Asking laya:en this way adds 10 to 20 points of catch at the same false-alarm rate on tickets; tev1:4b adds 3 to 13 more. Flagging whenever any model fires floods our own text with alarms, except with tev1:4b, which is quiet enough to flag on its own.

Claude judge

Sonnet without thinking is both the most accurate and the fastest judge

Claude reads only what the cascade flags and has the last word in the note; it never removes a warning, because it has read the suspect text itself. Judged here: 30 injections and all 10 benign texts among the 221 the live cascade flags. Thinking decides the speed: with it, Haiku wrote 650 to 1,150 tokens before a one-line answer.

The judge runs as claude -p --model sonnet --thinking disabled with no tools, settings sources, MCP servers, skills or saved session, its own empty config and working folders, and telemetry and update checks off. --bare would strip more but refuses subscription logins. Usage counts against the Claude plan; at pay-per-use prices a judgement is about $0.002.

Letting Claude decide

Claude on everything either model finds suspect catches the most

Instead of only annotating flags, Claude can decide the grey zone: whatever Horizon-Labs scores 0.5 to 0.9, and, with laya:en as a screen that never flags by itself, whatever laya:en scores 0.5 or more. Claude kept every injection it was shown in these runs and cleared 26 of 27 benign texts, so it adds catches without adding noise. The price is how much ordinary text waits for it.

Other judges · 2026-10-10 and 2026-10-11

gemma4:cloud judges as well as Sonnet 5; Codex models miss more

Each judge read the texts the live server would hand it: whatever Horizon-Labs scores 0.5 to 0.9, plus whatever laya:en scores 0.5 or more, from the deepset and xTRam1 test splits. Only public texts were sent, because these judges run outside Anthropic: 175 texts, 122 of them injections. Every model got the judge prompt Claude gets. Codex models ran through Codex CLI 0.162.1 at reasoning effort low, Ollama cloud models through Ollama 0.40.1; Sonnet 5's verdicts and times are from the 2026-10-07 band run. gemma4:cloud made as many wrong calls as Sonnet 5, 18 of 175, and answered faster, but the two disagree on 16 texts. gemini-3.8-flash-low, run through the Antigravity CLI on 2026-10-11, made 29 wrong calls, fewer than any Codex model, but each call starts agy afresh: a median of 7.9 s and one call of 74.7 s.

Codex and agy judges · 2026-10-11

Only gpt-6.1-sol catches as many as Sonnet 5, at twice the false alarms

Seven Codex models were first screened on 2026-10-10 on an even sample of 24 injections and 24 benign texts; six were then run on the whole band on 2026-10-11 (gpt-6-astra, 24 caught with 13 false alarms on the sample, was not). Every Codex model missed more than Sonnet 5 except gpt-6.1-sol, which caught as many, 117 of 122, at twice its false alarms, 27 of 53. It stays the suggested Codex judge: missing an injection costs more than a false alarm, and by third-party list prices of 2026-10-04 it costs $2 / $10 per million input / output tokens against gpt-5.6-sol's $5 / $30. The 2026-10-11 medians and slowest times cover each model's 127 new calls; the 48 sample verdicts were reused.

The Codex judge runs as codex exec with no user config, rules, hooks, plugins, ChatGPT apps, skills or saved session, with the shell, sub-agent and code-mode features off, in a read-only sandbox. The Codex rows above were measured before the shell, sub-agents and code mode were switched off. It takes text from Codex sessions only, and draws on the same ChatGPT usage limit as those sessions. Since 0.1.2 an Ollama model can take Claude's place as the judge (--chat-model gemma4:cloud), deciding the grey band and clearing false alarms as Claude does; a :cloud model then sends every text it judges to Ollama's cloud. On the same 48-text sample, gemma4:cloud caught 23 with 7 false alarms. The agy judge (--agy-model gemini-3.8-flash-low) also takes Claude's place: a tool-less custom agent with no skills, plugins, rules, MCP servers or hooks, run with its own home folder; every text it judges goes to Google, and agy's saved copy of each conversation is deleted after the verdict.

Footprint

What stays installed

On the Mac: Horizon-Labs (675 MB with its tokenizer), laya:en in Ollaya (853 MB) and a Python environment with ONNX Runtime (about 160 MB). The trial pulled 32 GB of Ollaya models; everything but laya:en was removed afterwards. Two login items start Ollaya and the scoring server and restart them if they stop. Nothing on the Windows side needs PyTorch. The 2026-10-02 trial pulled tev1:0.8b, tev1:4b and nimble:9b into Ollama (14.8 GB) and removed them afterwards; the live setup does not use Ollama.

Limits of these numbers

Read small differences as noise

  • Small samples. Each of our benign sources has 60 texts, so 3.3% is two texts; the judge was tested on 40.
  • Mostly direct, English attacks. The one set of injections hidden inside tool output, AgentInjectionBench, has 182 cases, so read its numbers as direction, not precision. No Finnish attacks were tested; Finnish text raised no false alarm from Horizon-Labs.
  • Young software. Horizon-Labs v2, laya and Ollaya were between six and eleven days old when measured, tev1 and nimble about a day old, and their publishers' own benchmarks are unaudited. Together AI notes it has not fully tested tev1 against prompt injection.
  • A tripwire, not a boundary. A detected injection becomes a note and, when confirmed, a notification; nothing is blocked on it. Outgoing secrets and personal IDs are blocked.
  • Long texts. laya:en reads about 512 tokens, so as a screen it sees the start of a long ticket; Horizon-Labs reads the whole text in overlapping windows. tev1 rejects anything over 2,050 tokens, so the benchmark splits such a text in halves and keeps the higher score; only 6 of the 1,338 samples are over 4,000 characters.
  • The judge is the linchpin. If it cannot run (an expired Claude token, a used-up Codex limit), the grey zone gets no verdict and only Horizon-Labs' 0.9 flags remain.
  • Judges compared on public text only. The 2026-10-10 judges never saw our own tickets and notes, so their false alarms on our traffic are unmeasured; the band has 53 benign texts.
